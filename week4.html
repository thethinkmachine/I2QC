The Three Generations of Quantum Computing

The term NISQ (Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum) was coined by John Preskill in 2018 to describe the current generation of quantum hardware: devices with 50–1000 qubits, but with errors that prevent running the deep circuits needed for textbook quantum algorithms (Shor, Grover at scale). Understanding where we are in the development of quantum computing is essential context for Week 4.

🔬 Generation 1 — Experimental Proof-of-Concept (Pre-2019)

Qubit count: 1–50 physical qubits

Key milestones: 1997 — NMR quantum computing (Chuang et al.); 2016 — IBM makes 5-qubit system publicly available via cloud; 2019 — Google claims "quantum supremacy" on 53-qubit Sycamore.

Limitations: Coherence times of microseconds to milliseconds. Gate fidelities insufficient for fault tolerance. Purpose: demonstrate QM principles, not solve useful problems.

⚡ Generation 2 — NISQ (Current, 2019–present)

Qubit count: 50–1000+ qubits (IBM Eagle: 127Q; IBM Condor: 1121Q; Google Willow: 105Q)

Characteristics: Qubits are noisy (error rates $\sim 0.1-1\%$ per gate). No quantum error correction. Circuit depth limited to $\sim 100-1000$ gate layers before noise overwhelms signal.

Algorithm class: Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQA) — designed to work with noise, keeping circuits short. Hybrid quantum-classical loops.

Goal: Find problems where NISQ devices provide any useful advantage over classical computers, even approximately.

🚀 Generation 3 — Fault-Tolerant QC (Future, post-~2030?)

Qubit count: Millions of physical qubits encoding thousands of logical qubits

Characteristics: Full quantum error correction implemented. Logical error rates exponentially suppressed. Can run arbitrarily deep circuits.

Algorithm class: Shor's algorithm, full Grover's at scale, quantum simulation of large molecules, quantum linear algebra.

IBM roadmap targets: 100+ logical qubits by ~2029 (Flamingo/Kookaburra processors). Google's Willow chip (2024) demonstrated below-threshold error correction with surface codes — a critical milestone.

Coherence Times: The Clock of Quantum Computing

The fundamental challenge of NISQ hardware is decoherence — the process by which a qubit's quantum state leaks into the environment, destroying the superposition and entanglement needed for computation. Two timescales characterize this:

ParameterPhysical MeaningEffect on QubitsTypical Value (IBM superconducting)
$T_1$ (relaxation time)Time for qubit to decay from excited state $|1\rangle$ to ground state $|0\rangle$Amplitude damping: $|1\rangle \to |0\rangle$ spontaneously100–500 μs
$T_2$ (dephasing time)Time for the relative phase between $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ to randomizePhase flip: $|0\rangle+|1\rangle \to |0\rangle-|1\rangle$ randomly50–300 μs (always $T_2 \leq 2T_1$)
Gate timeTime to execute a single 2-qubit gateLimits circuit depth before $T_1/T_2$ elapsed~100–500 ns

With $T_1 \approx 200$ μs and gate time $\approx 200$ ns, we can execute $T_1/t_{\text{gate}} \approx 1000$ gates before amplitude errors dominate. This sets the circuit depth limit for current NISQ devices.

Noisy Grover: A Cautionary Example

To illustrate the impact of noise, consider running Grover's algorithm on a real NISQ device. The ideal algorithm amplifies the target to probability 1. With noise at each gate, after $O(\sqrt{N})$ iterations:

  • Each Grover iteration applies $O(n)$ two-qubit gates, each with error rate $\epsilon \approx 0.5\%$
  • After $T = O(\sqrt{N})$ iterations: total error probability $\approx T \cdot n \cdot \epsilon = O(\sqrt{N} \cdot n \cdot \epsilon)$
  • For $n=20$ qubits ($N=10^6$), $T \approx 800$, error accumulates to $\sim 800 \times 20 \times 0.5\% = 80\%$. Basically random noise!

This is why NISQ algorithms use shallow circuits with small $T$ — variational algorithms in particular fix the circuit depth and let the classical optimizer compensate for noise.

The Variational Quantum Algorithm (VQA) Framework

Variational Quantum Algorithms are a family of hybrid classical-quantum algorithms specifically designed for NISQ hardware. The core insight is to use a fixed-depth parameterized quantum circuit as a function approximator and optimize its parameters classically, using the quantum device only to evaluate expectation values that are hard for classical computers.

The Cost Function

The cost function $C(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ must satisfy three properties for VQA to be viable:

📐 Three Properties of a Good VQA Cost Function
  1. Faithful (Problem Encoding): The minimum of $C(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ over all $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ corresponds to (or bounds) the solution of the original problem. If we're minimizing molecular energy, $C(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \langle\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})|H|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle$ where $H$ is the molecular Hamiltonian, and the variational principle guarantees $C(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \geq E_0$ (ground state energy) for all $\boldsymbol{\theta}$.
  2. Estimatable (Quantum Evaluable): $C(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ can be efficiently estimated using measurements on the quantum device. Typically $C$ is a sum of expectation values of Pauli strings: $C = \sum_k c_k \langle P_k\rangle$ where $P_k \in \{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}$. Each such term is estimated by repeated measurement in the appropriate basis.
  3. Trainable: The gradient $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} C$ can be computed (or approximated) efficiently so that classical optimizers can minimize $C$. This is the source of the barren plateau problem.

The Ansatz: Parameterized Quantum Circuits

The ansatz (German for "starting point" or "approach") is the parameterized quantum circuit $U(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ that prepares the trial state $|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle = U(\boldsymbol{\theta})|0\rangle^n$. Choosing a good ansatz is the central challenge in VQA design.

The Parameter-Shift Rule: Quantum Gradients

To minimize $C(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, we need gradients $\frac{\partial C}{\partial \theta_k}$. Unlike in classical neural networks (where backpropagation computes exact gradients), quantum circuits require a different approach: the parameter-shift rule.

📐 Parameter-Shift Rule

For a quantum circuit where gate parameter $\theta_k$ appears as $e^{-i\theta_k P/2}$ for a Pauli operator $P$:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial \theta_k} = \frac{C(\theta_k + \frac{\pi}{2}) - C(\theta_k - \frac{\pi}{2})}{2}$$

This is an exact analytical gradient, computed using only two circuit evaluations (one with $\theta_k$ shifted by $+\pi/2$, one by $-\pi/2$). It's the quantum analogue of finite differences but exact, not approximate.

For a circuit with $m$ parameters, computing the full gradient requires $2m$ circuit evaluations. For VQE on a molecule with 20 qubits and 1000 parameters, this means 2000 circuit evaluations per optimization step. This can be reduced using SPSA (Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation), which estimates the gradient from only 2 evaluations per step by perturbing all parameters at once.

VQA Challenges: Barren Plateaus and More

The Barren Plateau Problem

The barren plateau problem is the most serious obstacle to large-scale VQA. It refers to the phenomenon where the gradient of the cost function vanishes exponentially as the system size grows, making optimization infeasible.

💀 Barren Plateau: The Core Problem

For a random parameterized quantum circuit on $n$ qubits, the variance of the gradient scales as:

$$\text{Var}\left[\frac{\partial C}{\partial \theta_k}\right] \in O\!\left(\frac{1}{2^n}\right)$$

This means the gradient's magnitude is exponentially small in system size. The optimization landscape becomes exponentially flat — like trying to find the lowest point in the Sahara by bumping around randomly. With $n=50$ qubits, the gradient is $\sim 10^{-15}$ — undetectable by any finite number of measurements.

The barren plateau problem arises from the concentration of measure phenomenon in high-dimensional spaces. When the ansatz is expressible enough to explore the full unitary group (expressibility), random initialization places you in a region where the cost function is nearly constant in all directions.

Mitigation strategies under active research:

  • Local cost functions: Instead of a global cost, use a sum of local terms that only involve a few qubits each. Local costs can avoid exponentially small gradients.
  • Problem-inspired initialization: Start parameters near a known solution (e.g., UCCSD for chemistry), rather than random.
  • Layer-by-layer training: Add circuit layers gradually, using each layer's optimized parameters as initialization for the next.
  • Correlation-informed strategies: Understand the problem structure to design low-depth circuits that avoid barren regions.

Noise-Induced Barren Plateaus

Even without the high-expressibility issue, hardware noise introduces its own barren plateau. In the presence of noise with a constant error rate $\epsilon$ per gate, after $L$ layers of a circuit with $n$ qubits:

$$\text{Var}\!\left[\frac{\partial C}{\partial \theta_k}\right] \leq e^{-\Omega(\epsilon \cdot n \cdot L)}$$

The gradient decays exponentially in circuit depth $L$, qubit count $n$, and error rate $\epsilon$. This fundamentally limits how deep VQA circuits can be before noise makes optimization impossible — even if the hardware has perfect connectivity.

Measurement Efficiency

Computing the energy expectation value $\langle H\rangle$ for even a small molecule requires evaluating potentially exponentially many terms. For example, the hydrogen molecule H₂ has a Hamiltonian with $\sim 15$ Pauli terms; a 12-qubit molecule might have thousands.

📊 Key Measurement Techniques
  • Pauli string grouping: Group commuting Pauli terms and measure them simultaneously. Reduces total circuit executions from $O(K)$ to $O(K/c)$ for grouping factor $c$.
  • Shadow tomography: Use random measurements to estimate many observables from few circuit executions. With $\mathcal{O}(\log M)$ measurements, estimate $M$ observables simultaneously (Aaronson, 2018).
  • Classical shadows (Huang et al., 2020): Practical version of shadow tomography using random Clifford circuits. Scales favorably for local observables.

Error Mitigation on NISQ Hardware

Error mitigation is distinct from error correction: mitigation doesn't recover the quantum state, but uses classical post-processing to extrapolate what the noiseless result would have been. Key techniques:

📐 Zero-Noise Extrapolation (ZNE)

Execute the circuit at amplified noise levels (by stretching gate pulses or inserting gate-inverse pairs) and then extrapolate to zero noise. If the expectation value is $E(\lambda) = E_0 + c_1\lambda + c_2\lambda^2 + \ldots$ where $\lambda$ is the noise scale, running at $\lambda = 1, 2, 3$ lets us extrapolate $E(0) = E_0$.

📐 Probabilistic Error Cancellation (PEC)

Decompose the ideal (noiseless) channel as a quasi-probability distribution over implementable noisy channels. Sample from this distribution and compute a weighted average. The sampling overhead is $\gamma^{2n}$ where $\gamma = (1+\epsilon)$ is the one-norm of the quasi-probability, making it expensive for deep circuits.

VQA Applications: VQE, QAOA, qGANs

Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE)

VQE is the flagship application of VQA, targeting one of the most important computational challenges in science: finding the ground state energy of a molecular Hamiltonian. This underlies drug discovery, materials science, and quantum chemistry.

📐 The VQE Setup

Problem: Given a molecular Hamiltonian $H$ (a Hermitian matrix), find the ground state energy $E_0 = \min_\psi \langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle$ and the corresponding ground state $|\psi_0\rangle$.

Variational principle: For any trial state $|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle$:

$$\langle\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})|H|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle \geq E_0 \quad \text{for all } \boldsymbol{\theta}$$

So minimizing $C(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \langle H\rangle_\theta$ over all parameters gives an upper bound on $E_0$, and approaches $E_0$ as the ansatz becomes more expressive.

From Electrons to Qubits: Fermion Mappings

A molecule's electrons obey fermionic statistics: they must be antisymmetric under exchange (Pauli exclusion principle). This needs to be mapped to qubits, which have different algebraic properties. Three main mappings:

MappingIdeaQubit CostPauli String Weight
Jordan-Wigner (JW)Each spin-orbital = 1 qubit. Occupation number directly. Requires Z-string to maintain antisymmetry.$N$ qubits for $N$ spin-orbitals$O(N)$ — grows with system size
Bravyi-Kitaev (BK)Encodes both occupation and parity. Balances local and non-local info.$N$ qubits$O(\log N)$ — more efficient!
ParityStores cumulative parities. Two qubits can be tapered off by symmetry.$N-2$ qubits (after tapering)$O(N)$ some terms, $O(1)$ others

After mapping, the Hamiltonian becomes a sum of Pauli strings:

$$H = \sum_k c_k P_k, \qquad P_k \in \{I, X, Y, Z\}^{\otimes n}$$

For H₂ in a minimal basis with Jordan-Wigner mapping: $H$ has ~15 terms in a 4-qubit encoding. For larger molecules like BeH₂: $\sim$666 terms in 14 qubits. The ansatz used in VQE is often the Unitary Coupled Cluster Singles and Doubles (UCCSD), which is motivated by the quantum chemistry approximation of the same name.

Quantum GANs (qGANs)

Quantum Generative Adversarial Networks extend classical GANs to quantum settings. They consist of:

  • Quantum Generator: A parameterized quantum circuit $G(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ that produces a quantum state $|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle$. When measured, this produces a classical sample from the "learned distribution."
  • Classical Discriminator: A classical neural network $D$ that tries to distinguish samples from the generator from real data.
  • Training: Generator minimizes $\log(1-D(G(\boldsymbol{\theta})))$; Discriminator maximizes it. Classical adversarial training.
🏦 Application: Option Pricing (Finance)

A key application of qGANs is loading probability distributions into quantum states — the "data loading problem" that is hard classically. For pricing a European call option, we need the risk-neutral distribution of the underlying asset at expiry. A qGAN can learn to encode this distribution efficiently in a quantum state, enabling quantum Monte Carlo acceleration using Grover's algorithm for quadratic speedup in the integration. This is one of the most promising near-term financial applications.

Quantum Error Correction (QEC)

Quantum error correction is the theory and practice of protecting quantum information from the inevitable errors that occur during quantum computation. Without QEC, we are forever stuck in the NISQ era — a quantum computer without error correction is like a classical computer without error detection, operating in a world where cosmic rays flip bits randomly. QEC is what enables the third generation of fault-tolerant quantum computation.

DiVincenzo Criteria

Before discussing errors, let us recall what a quantum computer needs to be. David DiVincenzo (2000) formulated five criteria that any physical system must satisfy to implement quantum computation:

  1. Well-characterized qubits: The system must have a well-defined, scalable set of two-level quantum states.
  2. Ability to initialize: Qubits must be initializable to a simple state (usually $|0\rangle$).
  3. Long coherence times: The system must maintain quantum coherence longer than it takes to perform gates.
  4. Universal gate set: A set of quantum gates sufficient for universal quantum computation must be implementable.
  5. Qubit-specific measurement: The ability to measure individual qubits in the computational basis.

(For quantum communication, two additional criteria about conversion between stationary and flying qubits apply.) The hardest criterion in practice is criterion 3 — maintaining long coherence while implementing fast, precise gates.

Coherence Times and the Decoherence Problem

The environment surrounds every quantum system and continuously "measures" it through interactions, causing decoherence. The qubit loses its quantum superposition as the environment "learns" which state it's in. Mathematically, this is described by quantum channels (completely positive trace-preserving maps).

Why Classical Error Correction Doesn't Work

Classical error correction uses redundancy: encode $0 \to 000$ and $1 \to 111$, then take the majority vote after transmission. Simple and effective. Why can't we do the same for qubits?

⚠️ Three Quantum Obstacles to Naive Error Correction
  1. No-Cloning Theorem: We cannot copy an arbitrary quantum state, so classical repetition-code encoding $|\psi\rangle \to |\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle$ is impossible.
  2. Continuum of Errors: Quantum errors are not discrete bit-flips — a qubit can undergo a continuous rotation $R_x(\epsilon)$ for any tiny $\epsilon$. Classically we only have two possible errors (0 or 1); quantumly we have infinitely many.
  3. Measurement Destroys State: Classical error correction reads the codeword to identify errors. Measuring a quantum state collapses it — we need to detect errors without learning the encoded quantum state.

The solutions to these three obstacles are what make QEC theoretically elegant and practically challenging:

  1. Entanglement instead of copying: We entangle the logical qubit with ancilla qubits in a specific code structure. The code encodes one logical qubit into multiple physical qubits without violating no-cloning.
  2. Discretization of errors: Any quantum error can be decomposed in terms of Pauli operators $\{I, X, Y, Z\}$. QEC corrects a discrete set of errors (bit-flips $X$ and phase-flips $Z$), and the quantum state "snaps" to correctable states (digitization of errors).
  3. Syndrome measurement: We measure special observables called stabilizers that tell us which error occurred without revealing any information about the encoded state. The measurement result is the error syndrome.

The 3-Qubit Bit-Flip Code

The 3-qubit bit-flip code is the simplest quantum error correcting code. It is the direct quantum analogue of the classical 3-bit repetition code, adapted to handle quantum states correctly.

📐 Code Definition

Encode one logical qubit into three physical qubits:

$$|\bar{0}\rangle = |000\rangle \qquad |\bar{1}\rangle = |111\rangle$$

A general logical state $|\bar{\psi}\rangle = \alpha|\bar{0}\rangle + \beta|\bar{1}\rangle = \alpha|000\rangle + \beta|111\rangle$.

This code protects against a single bit-flip error (X gate applied to any one of the three physical qubits), provided the error probability $p < 1/2$.

Encoding Circuit

We start with $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ on qubit 0, and two ancilla qubits in $|0\rangle$:

Encoding Circuit (3-qubit bit-flip code): |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩+β|1⟩ ─── ●──── ●──── → Physical qubit 0 │ │ |0⟩ ──────────────── ⊕──── │──── → Physical qubit 1 │ |0⟩ ─────────────────────── ⊕──── → Physical qubit 2 Encoded state: α|000⟩ + β|111⟩ = |ψ̄⟩

Let us verify. Starting from $(\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle)|00\rangle$:

  • First CNOT (q0 → q1): $\alpha|000\rangle + \beta|110\rangle$
  • Second CNOT (q0 → q2): $\alpha|000\rangle + \beta|111\rangle = |\bar\psi\rangle$ ✓

Error Model and Syndrome Measurement

Assume each physical qubit independently undergoes a bit-flip error (X) with probability $p$ and no error with probability $1-p$. After the bit-flip channel, the encoded state $|\bar\psi\rangle = \alpha|000\rangle + \beta|111\rangle$ is acted on by one of $2^3 = 8$ possible error patterns. We only need to consider single-qubit errors (correction up to first order in $p$):

The syndrome is a pair of classical bits obtained by measuring the observables $Z_1Z_2$ (parity of qubits 1 and 2) and $Z_2Z_3$ (parity of qubits 2 and 3). These are measured without disturbing the encoded information:

Error$Z_1Z_2$ measurement$Z_2Z_3$ measurementSyndromeCorrection
None (I)+1+100None
$X_1$ (flip qubit 1)-1+110$X_1$
$X_2$ (flip qubit 2)-1-111$X_2$
$X_3$ (flip qubit 3)+1-101$X_3$

All four single-qubit error cases (including no error) give distinct syndromes! This is why we can uniquely identify and correct any single-qubit bit-flip error.

🔍 Why This Is Quantum-Legal

The syndrome measurement ($Z_1Z_2$ and $Z_2Z_3$) commutes with the logical operators ($\bar{X} = X_1X_2X_3$ and $\bar{Z} = Z_1$) and with the code subspace projector. Crucially, it gives us the error information WITHOUT revealing any information about the encoded state ($\alpha$ and $\beta$). This is the key insight: we measure the "error" but not the "data."

Logical Error Rate After Correction

Before correction: each qubit has error probability $p$. After correcting all single-qubit errors, a logical error occurs only if 2 or 3 qubits are flipped simultaneously. The logical error rate is:

$$p_{\text{logical}} = \binom{3}{2}p^2(1-p) + \binom{3}{3}p^3 = 3p^2 - 2p^3 \approx 3p^2 \text{ for small } p$$

For $p = 0.01$ (1% error): $p_{\text{logical}} \approx 3(0.01)^2 = 0.0003$. The 3-qubit code reduces the error rate by a factor of ~33! The requirement for this to help: $p_{\text{logical}} < p$, which gives $3p^2 < p$, i.e., $p < 1/3$. So the code helps whenever the physical error rate is below 1/3 (≈ 33%).

Phase-Flip Code, The Shor Code, and Beyond

The 3-Qubit Phase-Flip Code

The bit-flip code only corrects $X$ errors. A separate phase-flip code corrects $Z$ errors. In the Hadamard basis $\{|{+}\rangle, |{-}\rangle\}$, a Z error looks like a bit-flip (since $Z|{+}\rangle = |{-}\rangle$). So the phase-flip code encodes:

$$|\bar{0}\rangle = |{+}+{+}\rangle = \left(\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{\otimes 3}, \qquad |\bar{1}\rangle = |{-}{-}{-}\rangle$$

Then applying $X_1X_2X_3$ to measure $Z$-parity in the $X$ basis identifies and corrects single $Z$ errors.

The Shor Code: Correcting All Single-Qubit Errors

The Shor code (1995) was the first QEC code that corrects any single-qubit error — bit-flips ($X$), phase-flips ($Z$), combined errors ($Y = iXZ$), and even partial rotations. It concatenates the bit-flip and phase-flip codes:

$$|\bar{0}\rangle = \frac{(|000\rangle + |111\rangle)^{\otimes 3}}{2\sqrt{2}}, \qquad |\bar{1}\rangle = \frac{(|000\rangle - |111\rangle)^{\otimes 3}}{2\sqrt{2}}$$

This encodes 1 logical qubit into 9 physical qubits. Each block of 3 qubits protects against bit-flips; the larger structure across blocks protects against phase-flips.

📐 Discretization of Errors: Why Shor Works on All Errors

Any single-qubit error $E$ can be written as $E = e_0 I + e_1 X + e_2 Y + e_3 Z$. When the QEC syndrome is measured, the error "collapses" to one of the Pauli operators $\{I, X, Y, Z\}$ with probabilities $|e_0|^2, |e_1|^2, |e_2|^2, |e_3|^2$. The syndrome then perfectly identifies which Pauli occurred, and the appropriate correction is applied. The error is digitized — an analog rotation becomes a discrete correctable error!

The Quantum Hamming Bound

How many physical qubits do we need to encode 1 logical qubit and correct all $t$ single-qubit errors? The quantum Hamming bound gives a lower limit.

$$\text{Quantum Hamming Bound: } 2^n \geq (1 + 3n)\cdot 2^k$$

For encoding $k=1$ logical qubit and correcting $t=1$ error (any single-qubit error), we need:

$$2^n \geq 1 + 3n \implies n \geq 5$$

The minimum is $n=5$ physical qubits! The 5-qubit perfect code achieves this bound exactly — it is the smallest possible QEC code for correcting any single-qubit error. The Shor code (9 qubits) is not optimal in this sense, but was historically important as the first example.

✏️ Intuition for the Quantum Hamming Bound

With $n$ physical qubits, we have $2^n$ possible states. We need to accommodate $1 + 3n$ error patterns (no error: 1 pattern; $X, Y, Z$ on each of $n$ qubits: $3n$ patterns) for each of $2^k$ logical codewords. Each error pattern for each logical state must map to a distinct state (otherwise we can't distinguish them during syndrome measurement). This gives the bound $2^n \geq (1+3n)\cdot 2^k$.

The Path to Fault-Tolerant Quantum Computing

🗺️ IBM and Google Roadmaps

IBM Quantum roadmap (2024): The goal is to achieve 100 "error-free" logical qubits with quantum error correction by ~2029 using the Flamingo/Kookaburra processor families. Key milestone: demonstrating that adding more physical qubits reduces the logical error rate (showing "below threshold" behavior).

Google Willow (2024): The Willow chip (105 qubits) demonstrated for the first time that as the surface code distance $d$ increases from 3→5→7, the logical error rate decreases — confirming below-threshold operation and validating the feasibility of scaling to fault tolerance.

The threshold theorem is the theoretical foundation of fault-tolerant QC: if physical error rates are below a certain threshold (typically $\sim 0.1-1\%$ for the surface code), then logical error rates can be made arbitrarily small by using larger codes. Current hardware (IBM, Google) is approaching or crossing this threshold for 2-qubit gate fidelities.

The overhead, however, is enormous: the surface code (the leading candidate for fault-tolerant QC) requires $\sim 1000$ physical qubits per logical qubit at practical error rates. Running Shor's algorithm on a 2048-bit RSA number would need $\sim 4$ million physical qubits — many orders of magnitude beyond current capability. The path from today's 1000-qubit chips to millions of qubits requires breakthroughs in fabrication, control electronics, dilution refrigerators at scale, and more.
