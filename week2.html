Quantum Entanglement and Bell States

In Week 1, we introduced entanglement as a mathematical phenomenon: a multi-qubit state that cannot be written as a product of single-qubit states. In Week 2, we go much deeper — examining what entanglement means physically, how to create it, how to measure it, and how to weaponise it for quantum communication protocols.

What Entanglement Really Means

Consider two qubits that have interacted and are now in the maximally entangled state:

$$|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{|00\rangle + |11\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}$$

This state has a remarkable property: if Alice holds qubit 1 and Bob holds qubit 2, and Alice measures her qubit in the computational basis, she gets:

  • Outcome $|0\rangle$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, after which Bob's qubit instantly becomes $|0\rangle$
  • Outcome $|1\rangle$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, after which Bob's qubit instantly becomes $|1\rangle$

There is perfect correlation between their outcomes, regardless of how far apart they are. This was what troubled Einstein so deeply — he called it "spooky action at a distance" — but it has been confirmed experimentally beyond any doubt. Crucially, as we will see, it cannot be used to send information faster than light.

📐 The Four Bell States (Maximally Entangled 2-Qubit States)

The four Bell states form an orthonormal basis for the 2-qubit Hilbert space. They are the maximally entangled states:

$$|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{|00\rangle + |11\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad |\Phi^-\rangle = \frac{|00\rangle - |11\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}$$ $$|\Psi^+\rangle = \frac{|01\rangle + |10\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad |\Psi^-\rangle = \frac{|01\rangle - |10\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}$$

Maximally entangled means: measuring either qubit gives a completely random outcome (50/50), but the two outcomes are perfectly correlated (or anti-correlated).

Bell State Verification by Measurement

It is important to confirm that Bell states are truly entangled. For $|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$:

  • Measure qubit 0 in Z-basis: $p(0) = \frac{1}{2}$, $p(1) = \frac{1}{2}$. Completely random.
  • Conditional on qubit 0 = $|0\rangle$: qubit 1 collapses to $|0\rangle$ with certainty. Conditional on qubit 0 = $|1\rangle$: qubit 1 is $|1\rangle$. Perfect correlation.
  • Measure in X-basis: $|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{2}(|{++}\rangle + |{--}\rangle)$. Again random but perfectly correlated in X-basis too.

Bell Measurement

The Bell states $\{|\Phi^\pm\rangle, |\Psi^\pm\rangle\}$ form an orthonormal basis for the 2-qubit Hilbert space. A Bell measurement is a projective measurement in this Bell basis — it tells you which Bell state the two qubits are in.

🔑 Key Principle: Reversibility

Since the circuit that generates Bell states is unitary (and therefore reversible), the Bell measurement circuit is simply the reverse of the generation circuit: apply CNOT first, then Hadamard. The output (after measuring in the computational basis) tells you which Bell state you had.

Measuring: we get $00$ with certainty. ✓

🔍 Why Bell Measurement Matters

Bell measurement is the operational "decoder" for entanglement. Whenever we use a Bell state as a shared quantum resource and want to figure out what has happened (e.g. in teleportation), we perform a Bell measurement. The two classical bits output by the Bell measurement carry information about what correction is needed at the receiver's end.

Quantum Teleportation

Quantum teleportation sounds like science fiction, but it is a rigorously proven, experimentally verified quantum protocol. It allows Alice to send an unknown quantum state to Bob using only a shared Bell pair and two classical bits of communication — without the state ever physically traveling between them.

⚠️ Important: What Teleportation Is Not

Quantum teleportation does NOT:

  • Teleport matter or energy
  • Transmit information faster than light
  • Violate the No-Cloning Theorem

It teleports a quantum state. The original state is destroyed in the process (Alice's qubit collapses after measurement). Bob must receive Alice's 2 classical bits before he can recover the state — so no FTL communication occurs.

Setup

Three qubits involved:

  • Qubit 0 (Alice's): Contains the unknown state $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ to be teleported
  • Qubit 1 (Alice's half of EPR pair): Alice's portion of the shared Bell pair
  • Qubit 2 (Bob's): Bob's portion of the shared Bell pair

The initial state of all three qubits (qubits 1 and 2 in Bell state $|\Phi^+\rangle$):

$$|\text{total}\rangle_\text{start} = |\psi\rangle_0 \otimes |\Phi^+\rangle_{12} = (\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle)_0 \cdot \frac{|00\rangle + |11\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}_{12}$$ $$= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(\alpha|000\rangle + \alpha|011\rangle + \beta|100\rangle + \beta|111\rangle\bigr)_{012}$$

Full Protocol Derivation

1
Alice applies CNOT (qubit 0 controls qubit 1): $$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(\alpha|000\rangle + \alpha|011\rangle + \beta|110\rangle + \beta|101\rangle\bigr)$$
2
Alice applies Hadamard to qubit 0 ($H|0\rangle = |{+}\rangle$, $H|1\rangle = |{-}\rangle$): $$= \frac{1}{2}\bigl[|00\rangle(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle) + |01\rangle(\alpha|1\rangle+\beta|0\rangle) + |10\rangle(\alpha|0\rangle-\beta|1\rangle) + |11\rangle(\alpha|1\rangle-\beta|0\rangle)\bigr]$$
3
Alice measures qubits 0 and 1. She gets one of four outcomes, each with probability $\frac{1}{4}$:

The No-Cloning Theorem

Quantum teleportation raises an immediate question: didn't we just copy Alice's state $|\psi\rangle$ to Bob? No! Alice's qubit was destroyed in the measurement — it collapsed to a definite basis state. The state was moved, not copied. This is enforced by the:

📐 No-Cloning Theorem

There is no quantum operation that can make a perfect copy of an arbitrary unknown quantum state. Formally: there is no unitary $U$ such that $U|\psi\rangle|0\rangle = |\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle$ for all states $|\psi\rangle$.

Proof by contradiction: Suppose such a $U$ exists. Then:

$$U|0\rangle|0\rangle = |0\rangle|0\rangle \quad \text{and} \quad U|1\rangle|0\rangle = |1\rangle|1\rangle$$

By linearity of $U$, applying $U$ to the state $|{+}\rangle|0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)|0\rangle$:

$$U|{+}\rangle|0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(U|0\rangle|0\rangle + U|1\rangle|0\rangle) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle) = |\Phi^+\rangle$$

But a "clone" of $|{+}\rangle$ would be $|{+}\rangle|{+}\rangle = \frac{1}{2}(|00\rangle+|01\rangle+|10\rangle+|11\rangle)$. These are different! Contradiction. ∎

The CNOT gate might seem to clone: $\text{CNOT}|x\rangle|0\rangle = |x\rangle|x\rangle$ for $x \in \{0,1\}$. But this only works for basis states, not arbitrary superpositions. Applying CNOT to $|{+}\rangle|0\rangle$ gives the entangled state $|\Phi^+\rangle$, not the product state $|{+}\rangle|{+}\rangle$.

Quantum Interference

Interference is the third pillar of quantum computing's power (alongside superposition and entanglement). It is the quantum analogue of wave interference: amplitudes can add constructively or cancel destructively.

📐 Constructive and Destructive Interference

Consider two paths contributing amplitudes $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ to the same final state. The total amplitude is $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2$, and the probability is $|\alpha_1 + \alpha_2|^2$.

Constructive interference: If $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ have the same phase (both positive real, for instance), $|\alpha_1+\alpha_2|^2 = (|\alpha_1|+|\alpha_2|)^2 > |\alpha_1|^2 + |\alpha_2|^2$. The state is amplified.

Destructive interference: If $\alpha_1 = -\alpha_2$, then $|\alpha_1+\alpha_2|^2 = 0$. The state is suppressed.

The double-slit experiment is the classic demonstration of interference. A quantum particle (photon, electron) sent through two slits creates an interference pattern on a screen, even when sent one at a time — because the single particle's probability amplitude passes through both slits simultaneously and the amplitudes from each path interfere.

✏️ Interference in the Hadamard Transform

The Hadamard gate applied twice is the identity ($H^2 = I$). This is a direct consequence of interference. Start with $|0\rangle$:

$$H|0\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle + |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \equiv |{+}\rangle$$ $$H|{+}\rangle = H\cdot\frac{|0\rangle + |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{H|0\rangle + H|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} + \frac{|0\rangle-|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = |0\rangle$$

The $|1\rangle$ amplitudes: $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{-1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2} = 0$. Perfect destructive interference on $|1\rangle$! Meanwhile $|0\rangle$ experiences constructive interference. This is why $HH = I$.

The Intuition Behind Quantum Algorithm Construction

Now that we have all three pillars — superposition, entanglement, and interference — we can describe the general strategy for building quantum algorithms.

🏗️ The Universal Algorithm Design Pattern
  1. Superpose: Apply Hadamard gates to create a uniform superposition over all possible inputs: $H^{\otimes n}|0^n\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_{x=0}^{2^n-1}|x\rangle$. Now all $2^n$ inputs are "in the computer simultaneously."
  2. Entangle & Compute: Apply a quantum oracle (black-box function) that encodes the problem. This uses entanglement to create correlations between inputs and outputs.
  3. Interfere: Apply additional gates to create constructive interference on correct answers (high probability) and destructive interference on wrong answers (near-zero probability).
  4. Measure: Measure the output. With high probability, we observe the correct answer. Run multiple times ("shots") for statistical confidence.

This pattern underlies Deutsch-Jozsa, Bernstein-Vazirani, Grover's search, and even Shor's algorithm. The art of quantum algorithm design lies entirely in step 3 — finding the right interference pattern that amplifies the solution.

Practical Qiskit Programming

Now we get hands-on. We'll implement each of the quantum concepts we've learned — superposition, entanglement, and teleportation — as real Qiskit code.

1. Superposition Circuit (1-Qubit Hadamard)

The simplest meaningful quantum circuit: create a superposition and measure it many times to observe the 50/50 probability distribution.

from qiskit import QuantumCircuit # Create 1-qubit circuit qc = QuantumCircuit(1) qc.h(0) # Apply Hadamard: |0⟩ → |+⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 qc.measure_all() # Add measurement to all qubits qc.draw("mpl") # Visualize the circuit # Transpile to ISA (Instruction Set Architecture) circuit from qiskit.transpiler.preset_passmanagers import generate_preset_pass_manager pm = generate_preset_pass_manager(target=backend.target, optimization_level=3) qc_isa = pm.run(qc) # Execute on hardware (SamplerV2) from qiskit_ibm_runtime import SamplerV2 as Sampler sampler = Sampler(mode=backend) job = sampler.run([qc_isa], shots=1000) result = job.result() counts = result[0].data.meas.get_counts() # Expected: roughly {'0': 500, '1': 500}

What to expect: Due to superposition, the qubit collapses randomly to $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$ with equal probability at each measurement. Over 1000 shots, you'll see approximately 500 '0' and 500 '1', with small statistical fluctuations.

2. Entanglement Circuit (Bell State)

from qiskit import QuantumCircuit # Create 2-qubit Bell state |Φ+⟩ qc = QuantumCircuit(2) qc.h(0) # |00⟩ → |+0⟩ qc.cx(0, 1) # CNOT: |+0⟩ → (|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2 = |Φ+⟩ qc.measure_all() qc.draw() # Expected results: only '00' and '11' should appear (never '01' or '10') # This is the signature of entanglement: correlated outcomes

What to expect: On a perfect quantum computer, you'd see only '00' (50%) and '11' (50%). The fact that you never see '01' or '10' confirms the entanglement — the qubits are perfectly correlated. On real noisy hardware, you'll see small amounts of '01' and '10' due to errors.

3. Quantum Teleportation in Qiskit (Full Implementation)

This is the most complex circuit we've built so far. We implement the full 3-qubit teleportation protocol using Qiskit's classical feedback (if_test) for the conditional corrections.

import numpy as np from qiskit import QuantumCircuit # Define an unknown state with random angles theta = np.random.uniform(0, np.pi) varphi = np.random.uniform(0, 2*np.pi) # 3 qubits, 3 classical bits qc = QuantumCircuit(3, 3) # Step 0: Create Alice's unknown state on qubit 0 using U-gate qc.u(theta, varphi, 0.0, 0) # |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^{iφ}sin(θ/2)|1⟩ qc.barrier() # Step 1: Create EPR pair (Bell state) on qubits 1 and 2 qc.h(1) qc.cx(1, 2) qc.barrier() # Step 2: Alice's Bell measurement preparation qc.cx(0, 1) # CNOT: qubit 0 controls qubit 1 qc.h(0) # Hadamard on qubit 0 qc.barrier() # Step 3: Alice measures her two qubits qc.measure(0, 0) # qubit 0 → classical bit 0 qc.measure(1, 1) # qubit 1 → classical bit 1 # Step 4: Bob applies conditional corrections based on Alice's classical bits with qc.if_test((0, 1)): # If classical bit 0 is 1 → apply Z to qubit 2 qc.z(2) with qc.if_test((1, 1)): # If classical bit 1 is 1 → apply X to qubit 2 qc.x(2) # Validation: apply inverse of U-gate to qubit 2, then measure # If teleportation succeeded, this should give |0⟩ with certainty qc.u(theta, varphi, 0.0, 2).inverse() qc.measure(2, 2)
⚡ The if_test Feature

Qiskit's if_test implements dynamic circuits — quantum circuits where future gates depend on classical measurement outcomes. This is the quantum equivalent of a classical if statement and is essential for teleportation (and many other quantum protocols). It requires a backend that supports mid-circuit measurement and classical feedback, such as IBM's Eagle and Heron processors.

The IBM Quantum Patterns Workflow

IBM recommends structuring all quantum programs as four steps:

1
Map: Describe your problem as quantum circuits and quantum operators. This is the algorithm design phase.
2
Optimize (Transpile): Compile your ideal circuit into the hardware's native gate set, respecting qubit connectivity constraints. generate_preset_pass_manager does this. Higher optimization levels run slower but produce shorter circuits.
3
Execute: Submit the transpiled circuit to the backend using a Primitive (SamplerV2 or EstimatorV2). Specify the number of shots. The job runs and returns a PrimitiveResult.
4
Post-process: Extract counts, compute expectation values, plot histograms (plot_histogram), use marginal_counts to focus on specific qubits, and analyze the results.

🔍 Noise on Real Hardware

Real quantum hardware is noisy. The two main noise channels are:

  • T₁ (amplitude damping): The qubit spontaneously decays from $|1\rangle$ to $|0\rangle$. Typical IBM Q T₁ ≈ 100–300 μs.
  • T₂ (dephasing): The relative phase between $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ drifts. Typically T₂ ≤ T₁.
  • Gate errors: Each gate operation has a small error probability (typically 0.1–1% for 2-qubit gates).

This is why circuit depth (number of gate layers) is a critical resource — deeper circuits have more opportunities for errors to accumulate. The transpiler optimizes circuits to minimize depth on the target hardware.
